I get asked about this often enough that it's worth writing down. This isn't a universal formula —
just what I'd tell a past version of myself, ranked roughly by how much it actually mattered.

## 1. Advisor fit beats program prestige

Committees admit *to a lab*, not to a department. Before you write a single word of your statement:

- Read 3–5 recent papers from every advisor you're considering — not just the abstract, skim the method
  and discussion sections.
- Check whether they're actually taking students that cycle (departing/tenure-track/lab-size signals
  matter — email current students if you're unsure).
- Email advisors directly, 1–2 sentences: who you are, one specific thing about their recent work, and
  whether they expect to take students. Don't attach a CV unless asked.

## 2. The research statement is a fit document, not a highlight reel

The temptation is to summarize everything you've done. Resist it. A statement that works does three
things:

1. States a real, specific research question you care about — not "I'm interested in AI."
2. Shows evidence you can do research: a project, however small, with a method and a result, not just
   a topic.
3. Connects that question to *why this specific lab* — cite actual papers, actual methods.

## 3. Mechanics people forget

- **Recommendation letters**: give letter writers a one-page brief — your CV, the programs, and 2–3
  specific things you'd want mentioned. Do this 4+ weeks out.
- **Contact current students**, not just the advisor. They'll tell you things (funding stability,
  advising style, lab culture) that never make it into a faculty webpage.
- **Track everything.** A spreadsheet with deadlines, portal links, letter status, and advisor contact
  per school saves you in week 2 of application season.
- **Interviews**, when they happen, are mutual — come with real questions about lab structure, funding,
  and what a typical week looks like for their students.

## 4. What I'd underweight

GRE-equivalent scores, a long list of unrelated extracurriculars, and trying to sound like you already
know exactly what your dissertation will be. Committees are checking for research taste and trajectory,
not a finished plan.
